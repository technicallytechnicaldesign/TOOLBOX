ISO Fits & Tolerances

How to read a fit designation like ⌀25 H7/g6: hole-basis vs shaft-basis, what a tolerance grade (IT) actually sets, what the fundamental deviation letter does, and how the two combine into clearance, transition or interference. The preferred-fits table at the middle of this page is the one worth bookmarking.

Scope & standard This page follows ISO 286 (Parts 1 & 2) — the international system of limits and fits. The U.S. equivalent, ANSI/ASME B4.1, uses a related but different notation (lettered classes like RC, LC, LT, LN, FN rather than ISO's deviation-letter-plus-grade system) — see the comparison note under Preferred Fits if that's what your drawing actually calls out. Numeric values on this page are worked from the ISO 286 tolerance-grade formula for illustration, not copied from the published tables — for a real design, pull exact figures from ISO 286-1 or a fits calculator rather than this page.

Reading a Fit Designation

Everything about a fit is packed into one compact string. Once you can take it apart, every designation you meet afterwards reads the same way.

A basic size is shared by both mating parts. Each part then gets its own tolerance class: a letter (or two) that positions the zone relative to the basic size, followed by a number that sets how wide the zone is. Write the hole's class over the shaft's — by long-standing convention the hole's class always comes first — and you have a complete fit.

  1. ⌀ — diameter symbol, present when the toleranced feature is round.
  2. 25 — basic size, shared by both mating parts. Everything else is measured from here.
  3. H — the hole's fundamental deviation letter. H's lower deviation is exactly zero: the hole's smallest permitted size is the basic size.
  4. 7 — the hole's IT grade, setting how wide its tolerance zone is.
  5. g — the shaft's fundamental deviation letter. g sits just below zero, giving a small guaranteed clearance.
  6. 6 — the shaft's IT grade — often a step tighter than the hole's, since shafts are easier to hold to a precise size than holes are.

This example is a hole-basis fit: the hole carries the anchor letter H, and the shaft is toleranced to suit it. That's the overwhelmingly common choice in practice — a reamer, broach or gauge cut to one fixed size produces an H-class hole economically, while a shaft's diameter is comparatively cheap to adjust on a lathe or grinder to whatever the fit needs. A shaft-basis system runs the same logic in reverse (an h-class shaft, with varying hole classes) and shows up where one piece of standard shafting has to mate with several different housings — see Example E under Worked Examples.

Tolerance Grades (IT)

The grade number is pure width. It says nothing about where the zone sits — only how big it is.

ISO 286 defines eighteen grades, IT01 through IT18. A lower number is a tighter, more precise zone; a higher number is looser and cheaper to hold. The grades from IT5 to IT18 are generated from a single formula, so the system scales smoothly across every size instead of needing an arbitrary table entry for each one:

The generating formula i = 0.45 × ∛D + 0.001 × D — where D is the geometric mean (in mm) of the part's size bracket, and i comes out in micrometres. Each grade is then a fixed multiple of i. (Grades IT01, IT0 and IT1–4, for very fine work like gauge blocks, use their own formulas and aren't covered here.)

In practice, grade selection tracks process capability: roughly IT5–IT6 for precision-ground fits, IT7–IT9 for general machining, IT10–IT12 for coarser turning or drilling, and IT13 and up for cast, stamped or otherwise unmachined features and non-mating dimensions.

Fundamental Deviation Letters

Where the grade sets the width, the letter sets the position — how far the zone sits from the basic size, and in which direction.

For shafts (lowercase), the alphabet position says the direction and roughly the distance:

  • a–h — zero or negative deviation → clearance. h is the anchor: its upper deviation is exactly zero, so an h shaft's largest permitted size is the basic size itself.
  • js, j, k, m, n — deviation straddles or sits just past zero → transition. Depending where within its tolerance a given part lands, it may end up with clearance or a slight interference.
  • p–zc — positive deviation, growing through the alphabet → interference, from a light locational press up to a heavy shrink fit.

Holes (uppercase) mirror this exactly, reflected about zero: H is the anchor, with its lower deviation at zero, so an H hole's smallest permitted size is the basic size. A–H run clearance-direction (looser as the letter moves away from H), JS/J/K/M/N transition, P–ZC interference.

Clearance, Transition & Interference

Put a hole's zone and a shaft's zone on the same zero line and their relative position tells you exactly what kind of fit you get.

Clearance

The shaft's zone sits entirely below the hole's. Every possible pairing has a gap — the parts always assemble by hand.

Transition

The zones overlap. Depending where each part actually lands within its tolerance, an individual pair might clear slightly or interfere slightly.

Interference

The shaft's zone sits entirely above the hole's. Every possible pairing overlaps in material — assembly needs force, heat, or both.

Worked Examples

Decoding full designations, plus one fully worked numeric limit calculation.

A — ⌀25 H7/g6, Worked Numerically

  1. Grade: at 25 mm (18–30 mm bracket, D≈23.2), the grade formula gives i≈1.31μm — IT7 = 16i≈21μm, IT6 = 10i≈13μm.
  2. Hole (H7): lower deviation is 0 by definition → 25.000–25.021.
  3. Shaft (g6): g's fundamental deviation works out to roughly −7μm, and the zone is 13μm wide → 24.980–24.993.
  4. Result: clearance ranges from 0.007 (tightest) to 0.041 (loosest) — always positive, so this is a guaranteed clearance fit.

B — ⌀40 H11/c11

  1. Grade IT11 on both parts — wide, commercial-grade zones on hole and shaft alike.
  2. c sits far into clearance territory — the largest deviation letter before the transition group starts.
  3. Category: loose running fit — generous clearance, tolerant of dirt, heat, or minimal-precision production.

C — ⌀12 H7/k6

  1. k is a transition letter — its zone sits just past zero, overlapping the hole's.
  2. Category: locational transition. Depending on where each part actually lands, an individual assembly might have a whisper of clearance or a whisper of interference.
  3. Use: accurate location that's still meant to be dismantled occasionally — a located bushing or a bearing housing, for instance.

D — ⌀60 H7/u6

  1. u is deep in interference territory — its zone sits entirely above the hole's.
  2. Category: force fit. Every possible pairing interferes; assembly needs a press, heat to expand the hole, or both.
  3. Use: parts meant to behave as one piece once assembled — a gear hub shrunk onto a shaft.

E — ⌀30 K7/h6, Shaft-Basis

  1. h is the anchor letter, but here it's on the shaft — this is a shaft-basis fit, the reverse of every example above.
  2. K, a transition-family hole letter, is toleranced to suit the fixed shaft rather than the other way around.
  3. Why: a common scenario is one length of precision shafting (held to h6 everywhere) mating with several different housings bored to different classes — cheaper than re-holding the shaft for each one.
