Welding Symbols

How the AWS A2.4 welding symbol is built, how to tell arrow side from other side, what each basic weld glyph means, where size and length attach to it, and the supplementary marks that ride along — weld-all-around, field weld, contour and finish. Worked examples at the bottom decode full callouts start to finish.

Scope & standard This page follows AWS A2.4 — the U.S. structural/general welding-symbol standard — for structure and terminology, with the occasional note on where ISO 2553 (the international counterpart) differs. The marks below are simplified teaching pictograms, not typographically exact reproductions of the standard's figures. For contract drawings, WPS work, or anything code-governed, read the current edition of the governing standard itself — and put its name and edition on the drawing (see the Weldment Review checklist for the rest of that list).

Anatomy of a Welding Symbol

Every part of the symbol has a fixed job and a fixed address. Learn the layout once and every callout you meet afterwards reads the same way.

The whole thing is built on a horizontal reference line. An arrow leans off one end and touches the joint being welded. Marks riding the line say what kind of weld and how big; whether a mark sits above or below the line says which side of the joint it belongs to. A tail — a short fork at the line's far end — carries anything that doesn't fit on the line itself: welding process, a spec, a note. No tail is drawn at all when there's nothing to put in it.

  1. Joint — the actual parts being welded. The arrow touches it; everything else is annotation.
  2. Arrow — connects the reference line to the joint. Its far end can bend or point to a specific member when it matters which one.
  3. Reference line — the spine every other mark attaches to.
  4. Weld symbol, arrow side — below the line. A mark above the line would call the weld on the other side instead — see the next section.
  5. Size — leg length, groove depth, or diameter, written to the left of the weld symbol.
  6. Length & pitch — written to the right; a dash-separated pair means intermittent (length–pitch).
  7. Tail — process, spec or note. Omitted entirely when empty.

Weld-all-around and field-weld marks also live right at the arrow/line junction when they're used — they're introduced on their own in Supplementary Symbols so this base diagram stays readable.

Arrow Side vs. Other Side

This is the rule the rest of the symbol depends on, and the one that trips people up first.

Below the reference line = the weld is on the arrow side — the same side of the joint the arrowhead touches. Above the reference line = the weld is on the other side — the side opposite the arrow, which may be facing away from you in the view you're looking at. A mark on both sides of the line means the joint is welded on both sides.

Below the line — arrow side

Symbol hangs below the line → weld goes where the arrowhead points.

Above the line — other side

Symbol sits above the line → weld goes on the far side, opposite the arrow.

Two more placement rules ride along with this one. A mark drawn centred on (straddling) the reference line, rather than above or below it, is used for weld types that don't have an arrow-side/other-side distinction to begin with — spot, seam and projection welds are the common case, since the parts are simply squeezed together rather than joined from one particular face. And on a joint like a T or a lap, the two possible weld locations can look identical in a single view — there's often no other cue in the drawing to tell them apart. Arrow side / other side placement is the symbol's way of resolving that, not a formality. ISO 2553 uses the same above/below logic, so once you know which standard's tail conventions and finish letters you're reading, the placement rule itself doesn't change underneath you.

Basic Weld Symbols

The mark says what kind of weld. Each one below is drawn in its canonical orientation — sitting above an implied reference line running under its bottom edge — the same shape flips below the line for an arrow-side call, as in the examples further down.

Fillet

The workhorse: two members meeting at roughly a right angle, weld metal filling the inside corner. Sized by leg length unless the drawing says otherwise.

Square Groove

Both edges left square, butted together over a root opening. Thin material, single or double-sided pass.

V-Groove

Both edges bevelled to form a V. The default full-penetration groove for plate too thick to fuse through from a square edge.

Bevel Groove

Only one member is bevelled; the other stays square — common where one side can't be chamfered, like a machined face or a casting.

U-Groove

Both edges gouged to a rounded profile. Less filler metal than a V for the same thickness, at the cost of more prep.

J-Groove

One member gouged round, the other left square — the U-groove's bevel-groove counterpart.

Flare-V Groove

Between two members whose own curvature forms the groove — two round bars laid together, for instance. No machining of the edge itself.

Flare-Bevel Groove

One flat member against one curved member — a bar against a plate, a pipe into a flat face.

Plug / Slot

A hole in the top member — round for plug, elongated for slot — filled with weld metal down to the member below.

Spot / Projection

A fused nugget through overlapped sheet, no continuous seam. Resistance spot welding's symbol; also covers projection welding.

Seam

A continuous line of fused nuggets along overlapped sheet — same circle glyph as spot; width, length and pitch in the callout say which one you're reading.

Backing

A bar, strip or pass of weld metal on the back of a groove weld, placed before welding to support and contain the root pass.

Surfacing

Weld metal built up on a surface rather than joining two parts — hardfacing, cladding, or dimensional build-up on a worn part.

A few less common members of the family aren't diagrammed here: edge and corner-flange welds have their own symbols in A2.4 for formed sheet-metal edges, and scarf joints (mostly brazing) have theirs. Rare enough outside sheet-metal and specialty work that they're better looked up in the standard when you actually hit one.

Sizing, Length & Pitch

The weld symbol says what kind of weld. Everything positioned around it says how big and how often — and each value has a fixed address, because a number on the wrong side of the symbol changes what's specified.

ValueWhere it's writtenReads as
SizeLeft of the weld symbolFillet leg length, groove depth, or plug/slot width
Groove angleNested at the mouth of the groove symbolIncluded angle between the prepared faces
Root openingAt the root (point) of the groove symbolGap left between the faces before welding
LengthRight of the weld symbolLength of a single weld or weld segment
PitchRight of the length, separated by a dashCentre-to-centre spacing for an intermittent weld, e.g. 2-4 = 2″ welds on 4″ centres
Number of weldsIn parentheses, right of the symbolCount of spot, plug, or projection welds — e.g. (5)

Exact typography for edge cases — stacked dimensions, unusual combinations — is worth checking against A2.4's own placement figures rather than guessing from the pattern above.

Supplementary Symbols

Marks that modify a weld symbol rather than naming a weld type. They sit right at the arrow/reference-line junction (weld-all-around, field weld) or ride alongside the weld symbol itself (melt-through).

Weld All Around

A circle at the arrow/reference-line junction: the weld runs all the way around the joint, not just where the arrow points.

Field Weld

A filled flag at the junction: this weld is made at the installation site, not in the shop — useful for sequencing and for costing site labour separately.

Melt-Through

Shares its glyph with surfacing — a shallow dome — placed on the other side of a groove weld to call for visible, controlled penetration showing through.

A consumable insert (a filler ring left in the root of a groove weld) is normally called out by note or in the tail rather than by a distinct glyph on most shop drawings — worth confirming against the standard if you're drafting one from scratch.

Contour & Finish

Contour describes the final profile of the weld face; the finish letter beside it says how that profile gets there. Both sit directly above the weld symbol they apply to.

Flush

A straight line: the weld face is dressed level with the surrounding surface.

Convex

An outward arc: the weld face bulges above the surrounding surface.

Concave

An inward arc: the weld face dishes below the surrounding surface — typical for a finished structural fillet.

LetterFinish method
CChipping
GGrinding
MMachining
RRolling
HHammering
PPeening

No letter shown means the finish method is the welder's choice — but the contour itself is still mandatory once it's on the drawing.

Worked Examples

Full callouts, decoded left to right — the same reading order you'd use on a real drawing.

A — Intermittent Fillet

  1. Type: fillet weld.
  2. Side: below the line — arrow side.
  3. Size: 1/4″ leg.
  4. Pattern: 2″ long welds on 4″ centres (intermittent).
  5. Tail: none — process left to the shop.

B — V-Groove, Other Side

  1. Type: V-groove weld.
  2. Side: above the line — other side.
  3. Groove angle: 60° included.
  4. Root opening: 1/8″.
  5. Tail: GTAW — process specified.

C — Fillet with Supplementary Marks

  1. Type: fillet weld, other side, 1/4″ leg.
  2. Circle at the vertex: weld all around the joint.
  3. Flag at the vertex: field weld — made on site, not in the shop.

D — Spot Weld, 5 Places

  1. Type: spot weld — circle straddles the line, no arrow-side/other-side distinction.
  2. Size: 0.25″ diameter.
  3. Count: 5 places.

E — Fillet, Both Sides, Unequal Legs

  1. Type: fillet weld, both sides of the joint.
  2. Arrow side: 3/16″ leg (below the line).
  3. Other side: 1/4″ leg (above the line) — each side sized independently.
